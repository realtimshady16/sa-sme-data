# Entrepreneurship as a jobs strategy: what the public data says

*Brief, 2026-11-23. Sources and provenance in §8; everything below is re-derivable from the scripts listed there.*

## 1. The claim under test

South African policy treats new businesses as the answer to unemployment. That is a factual claim, and the public record is now good enough to test it. It fails — not because entrepreneurship is unimportant, but because the two things being conflated, *starting a business* and *employing people*, barely overlap in the data.

## 2. What the country's businesses actually look like

There are **1.98 million businesses not registered for VAT** and **1.4 million companies expected to file returns** — different populations, measured by different instruments, with almost nothing linking them.

The informal population, from Stats SA's own unit records (SESE 2023):

| | |
|---|---|
| Businesses | 1 978 211 |
| Median annual turnover | **R16 000** (R19 500 in 2009 prices — no real improvement in fourteen years) |
| Share that disclose turnover under R50 000 a year | **76.4%** |
| **Share that employ nobody at all** | **85.7%** |
| Paid jobs per 100 businesses | **26** |

Three quarters of them exist because there was no alternative: **74.6%** of owners cite unemployment, no other income, retrenchment, "to survive/make ends meet", or earning while job-searching. Only 22.3% started on an opportunity.

The formal side is thin at the bottom too: of 1 228 437 companies assessed for tax year 2023, **54% declared nil taxable income and 24% a loss**, and **1 195 companies — one in a thousand — pay two-thirds of all company tax**. The register is not a business count: **2.77 million of its 4.14 million entities are not expected to file at all (66.8%).**

## 3. The jobs come from survivors, and mostly from the desperate ones that survived

Employment in the informal sector is a function of **age**, not intent (SESE 2023):

| Business age | % that employ anyone | Paid jobs per 100 |
|---|---|---|
| Under a year | 10.9% | **7.0** |
| 1–3 years | 10.0% | 20.0 |
| 3–5 years | 15.2% | 27.1 |
| 5–10 years | 13.2% | 28.3 |
| 10+ years | **20.9%** | **42.9** |

Firms older than five years hold **52% of employers and 60% of paid jobs**; businesses under a year hold 12.4% of employers but **4.4% of the jobs**. A new informal business, statistically, employs almost nobody.

The motive result is the one that should change minds, because both halves are true. **Per business**, an opportunity-started firm is 1.8× as likely to employ (22.3% vs 12.2%) and carries 1.85× the jobs per 100 (41.1 vs 22.2). **In aggregate**, necessity-started firms supply **64% of all informal paid jobs** — because they are three-quarters of the population. "Only opportunity entrepreneurs create jobs" is true firm by firm and false for the economy.

And the motive gap is mostly a survival effect: at birth, opportunity-started businesses are five times as likely to be employers (35.0% vs 6.6%); by ten years it is 24.4% vs 20.4%. **Hiring is something informal businesses grow into.** Motive predicts the speed, not the ceiling.

The trend points the same way, and harder. Across four surveys: the informal sector grew **64% between 2009 and 2023**, but from 2013 to 2023 the number of businesses rose 24% while **employers fell 27% and paid jobs fell 25%**. The employer rate halved from its 2013 peak, to 14.3%. In real terms the median business turns over less than it did in 2009. The one improving number is motive — necessity starts fell from 68% to 56% — which suggests entry is not the binding constraint; survival is.

## 4. Survival is remarkably poor, and now measurable

A cohort of VAT-registered businesses followed by SARS for ten years shows **53.6% survival to 2024/25** — roughly **6.7% leaving the register every year** — while the register grew 38% and the active base 12.5%. Formality is not a one-way escalator.

## 5. What the state actually does with its money

Programme-level spending, 2026/27: the Small Business Development vote is **R3 036.5 million**, 71% of it transfers, of which **R984.5 million** goes to the merged Small Enterprise Development and Finance Agency. Two structural facts:

- **The only fast-growing line is aimed at the informal tier** — Integrated Cooperatives and Micro Enterprise Development went from R142.7m (2024/25) to R532.2m, a 3.7× rise in two years — while the growth-facing Enterprise Development programme **fell 5.1% a year in nominal terms** to 2025/26.
- SEDFA is mostly a payroll: across the MTEF, **40.7% compensation of employees and 49.7% goods and services, against 7.6% transfers**. Its ~R1.8bn of small-business lending is repaid, not spent.

Meanwhile **R8.4 billion** runs through the tax code (R3.9bn small-business-corporation relief plus R4.5bn employment tax incentive), against a R3.0bn department: the state spends roughly three times more on small business through the tax system than through the ministry responsible for it. Neither vote contains a line that funds equity for new firms, and the only instrument that did (s12J venture-capital relief, R1.0–1.3bn a year) no longer carries an estimate.

Procurement is the largest unrealised lever: of **R328.66bn** of national and provincial spend in FY2025/26, **36.05% went to suppliers with turnover under R50m**, 30.97% to large business, and **32.98% is unclassifiable because no turnover is recorded**. That is a share of spend, not evidence that any set-aside was applied.

## 6. The hole in the evidence, stated plainly

**There is no public data linking a formal firm to the number of people it employs**: the company tax return has no employment field, the Quarterly Employment Survey reports by industry and establishment, and the wage subsidy is claimant-only. There is also **no panel**, so survival curves by entry cohort cannot be computed. The causal claim is therefore untestable from public data, and anything stronger than the associations in §3 is unsourced.

The one artefact that would change this is a flow table SARS already holds: **VAT registrations and deregistrations by year and turnover band**. It would give births, deaths and net in a single file, and it would let the "70–80% of small businesses fail" line that everyone repeats be checked for the first time.

## 7. What follows

If the goal is jobs rather than business starts, the data points away from entry subsidies and towards **survival past year five and the R50k–R250k turnover band** (14% of informal businesses, 37.5% of their paid jobs), where employers are 3× as common as in the micro tail. It also flags the sharpest open question: businesses with a licence or an income-tax number carry 3–4× the jobs per 100 of the sector average, but a cross-section cannot tell whether formalisation enables hiring or hiring forces formalisation.

One upcoming test is free. From 1 April 2026 the VAT registration threshold rises from R1m to **R2.3m**, and **47.8% of active VAT vendors sat below it**. The measured formal population will roughly halve in definition, and 2026/27 becomes a natural experiment on whether firms that leave the VAT net growth faster (less compliance cost) or slower (their customers lose the input-VAT reclaim). The 2027 edition of Tax Statistics is the measurement.
